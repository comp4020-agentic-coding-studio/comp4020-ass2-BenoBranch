industry: Session musicians & commercial composition
pitch: >-
  Commercial composition --- jingles, underscore, temp-track replacement, the
  twelve bars behind a livestream --- is pattern completion with a deadline.
  Generative audio models now produce broadcast-ready backing tracks, stems,
  and full arrangements in the house style of any reference a client uploads,
  cleared and licensed at the point of generation, in less time than it takes
  a session player to find parking near the studio. A composer's overnight
  turnaround becomes a five-minute one. The freelance session pool that used
  to be booked by the hour becomes a subscription line item.
migrationPath: >-
  Briefs are submitted as a reference track plus mood tags; the system returns
  stem-separated, royalty-cleared arrangements in the target genre, with
  revision handled by re-prompting rather than re-booking a session.
breakingChanges:
  economic: >-
    Session musicianship was never a single job but a long tail of working
    musicians who stitched a living from jingle dates, film-score overdubs,
    wedding-band gigs, and the occasional tour, cross-subsidizing rehearsal
    time and instrument upkeep across all of it. Removing the
    commercial-composition slice doesn't just cut a fee, it removes the
    liquidity that made the rest of that patchwork income solvent, and the
    players most exposed are the ones with the least ability to specialize
    their way into the remaining human-differentiated work --- because that
    differentiation is itself built from years of paid commercial reps they
    can no longer get. The pitch prices a single track. It doesn't price the
    career built on a hundred tracks like it.
  functional: >-
    A generated backing track has no room to be redirected mid-take --- no
    producer saying "give me less on the snare and more attitude on the
    outro" and getting an answering musical idea back, because there's no
    player listening to the room and responding to a request that wasn't
    fully specified in words. Commercial work was also where working
    musicians developed the chops --- sight-reading under pressure, playing
    to a click, taking a note and using it --- that carried into every other
    kind of playing they did. Remove the commercial tier and the training
    ground for the whole profession goes with it, not just the fraction of
    hours it directly paid for.
rollbackPlan: >-
  Individual clients can still book a human player directly, and reframing
  "played by a person" as a premium credit could rebuild some paid demand ---
  but the dense local pool of working musicians that made same-day session
  calls possible took decades of overlapping paid work to sustain, and
  doesn't reconstitute just because demand ticks back up later. Call this
  plan real but slow, and slower than anyone financing the migration will
  wait for.

Notice this week is longer in one place than any notice so far: breaking
changes now runs past the pitch that provoked it. That's not a formatting
accident carried over from earlier weeks --- it's the course's own claim
about where the industries in this half of the semester sit. Clerical and
mechanical work (weeks 2--6) had breaking-changes sections that were serious
but contained. Creative and care work doesn't hold still the same way, and
the notice format is being asked to show that, not just say it.

## Outline

- why this week's rollback plan is "real but slow" instead of a clean yes or
  a flat no --- the first notice that isn't at either extreme
- what "the pitch prices a single track, not the career built on a hundred
  tracks like it" is doing as a sentence, and why it sits in the economic
  half rather than the functional half
- how to tell, reading only the pitch and migration path, that this is a
  worse case for a rollback plan than week 1's directory-assistance notice
  was --- before you get to the breaking-changes section that confirms it
